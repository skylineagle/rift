criticalOverload silently drops data, and TCP streams arrive spliced

Rift never pushes back on a fast sender. When the outbound side cannot keep up, the bridge drops whole 4 KB read chunks (try_send into a 256-slot queue). With an unthrottled TCP sender, 70 to 85% of the bytes are thrown away, and the TCP receiver gets a byte stream with holes cut at arbitrary offsets. Any protocol on top sees corruption, not just a gap. A direct TCP connection on the same machine moves the same load at 2.7 GB/s with zero loss, because TCP flow control slows the sender instead.

highLoss starts well below the ceiling, in bursts

The queue holds about 1 MB, roughly 5 ms of traffic at 200 MB/s, so any short stall loses data. Several directions are clean at 300 MB/s but lose 1 to 7% at 200 or 400 MB/s in the same sweep. It depends on timing, not on rate. Treat about 100 MB/s per direction on TCP and about 50 MB/s on UDP as the reliably clean envelope on this machine.

mediumAbout one CPU core per 100 MB/s

At 100 MB/s TCP→TCP Rift uses about 90 to 95% of one core, and it saturates at 3 to 3.5 cores when overloaded. Most of the cost is per-chunk work done even with --quiet: each chunk is copied into two event payloads and broadcast to the log subscriber.

mediumUDP tops out at about 200k datagrams per second

Small datagrams hit a packet-rate limit: 64 B datagrams deliver only about 13 MB/s, and 256 B datagrams lose 5 to 13% at 50 MB/s. Large datagrams scale well: 8 KB datagrams reach 1.1 to 1.3 GB/s. A UDP source that feeds a UDP-server side, which fans out to every known peer, is the weakest path.

mediumMemory is small at rest but spikes under overload

Rift idles at about 8 MB RSS and stays under 15 MB at moderate rates. Overloaded runs peak at 100 to 400 MB as queues, broadcast rings and the allocator fill. The soak below checks whether it comes back down and stays flat over hours.

mediumDrop reports and logs are incomplete under load

Drop notifications and data logs share one broadcast channel (4096 events). When the printer lags, tokio skips the oldest events, and dropped N chunks notifications go with them. In overloaded runs Rift reported a fraction of the chunks it actually discarded. The log is not an audit trail at high rates.

mediumSIGTERM is ignored while stdin is an open pipe

rift pipe with stdin connected to a pipe that never closes does not exit on SIGTERM: it was still alive after 3 s and needed SIGKILL. With stdin on /dev/null it exits at once. This affects docker run -i and supervisors that pipe stdin.

good110-minute soak: no leaks, flat memory, no crash

One rift run process carried 7 flows for 110 minutes on macOS: TCP 40 MB/s each way, UDP 10 MB/s each way, UDP→TCP 10 MB/s each way, plus a TCP client reconnecting every second. That is about 120 MB/s, roughly 830 GB in total. RSS stayed between 17 and 36 MB and ended at 19 MB. Threads stayed at 12 and file descriptors at 45 through about 6,600 reconnects. CPU ran at 150 to 180% of one core. Steady-state loss was about 0.004% per flow, all of it in short stalls that also hit a direct TCP control flow, so they came from the host.

At minute 62 the host ran out of network buffers (ENOBUFS), which also broke the control connection. Rift's TCP-client side lost its peer and kept retrying, which is correct. That profile then sat idle only because the harness accepts a single connection. The other profiles carried on unaffected.

goodLight-load latency is low and stable across every combination

At 1 MB/s every side combination has a median one-way latency of about 110 to 130 µs and a p99 of 180 to 330 µs. That is about 50 µs over a direct loopback connection, since Rift adds one kernel round trip. Nothing reordered frames in any run.

noteUART was measured on Linux only

On macOS, serialport sets the baud rate through IOSSIOSPEED, which pseudo-terminals reject with Not a typewriter, so a pty cannot stand in for a serial port. UART combinations ran on Linux, where ptys accept termios. A pty is not baud-limited, so those numbers are Rift's serial-path ceiling. A real 115200 baud link carries about 11.5 KB/s, and anything faster from a network side overflows Rift's queue and is dropped. On Linux, serial→network stays clean up to about 60 MB/s, while network→serial loses 74 to 90% of the data once overloaded.

noteLinux ran on a 2 vCPU VPS

The Linux machine is a small cloud VM: 2 vCPUs on an AMD EPYC 9354P, shared by Rift and the harness. Its numbers are an edge-box profile, not a like-for-like comparison with the 11-core M3 Pro. Clean throughput there is about 10 to 50 MB/s per TCP direction and 1 to 10 MB/s for UDP sources. The 60-minute soak at the same 120 MB/s mix was overloaded from the first minute and delivered about 2 to 10 MB/s per flow with heavy loss. Rift itself survived the full hour with RSS flat near 40 MB. The 2 MB/s serial profile in that soak stayed almost lossless.

What was tested

A purpose-built harness (bench/, Rust, standalone crate) runs the shipped rift release binary as a child process and plays the outside party on both sides. It writes sequence-numbered frames into one side and reads them from the other.

  • Side types: tcp-server, tcp-client, udp-server, udp-client, uart. All 15 unordered pairs, including same-type pairs, in both directions: 25 directed combinations, 16 on macOS.
  • Outer peers: TCP connect or accept, UDP sockets with 7 MB buffers (a hello datagram registers the harness with a udp-server side), and a raw pseudo-terminal pair standing in for the UART (uart:///dev/pts/N?baud=115200).
  • Frames: magic, length, sequence, send timestamp, a filler byte pattern and a trailing sequence. The receiver re-syncs on the magic, so it tolerates re-chunking. It counts delivered, lost (sent minus delivered), out-of-order and corrupt frames (a torn or spliced frame fails the filler or tail check), and records per-frame latency in an HDR histogram.
  • Rate sweep: paced sender at fixed MB/s steps, then unthrottled. 1 KB frames, 4 s per step, a fresh rift pipe process per run.
  • Bidirectional: both directions unthrottled at once, per pair.
  • Frame size: 64 B to 8 KB, at 100 MB/s and unthrottled, on four representative directions. The loopback UDP datagram limit on macOS is 9216 bytes.
  • Logging: --log json --quiet (the harness default), plus --log json|hex|text printing every chunk to /dev/null.
  • Resources: RSS, CPU time, thread count and open file descriptors of the Rift process, every 100 ms (1 s in the soak), via proc_pidinfo on macOS and /proc on Linux.
  • Soak: one rift run --all process with several profiles carrying sustained traffic both ways for hours, plus a TCP client that reconnects every second to catch leaks.

Caveats. On each platform everything runs on one machine over loopback, so the harness competes with Rift for cores, and an iOS simulator and system scans ran in the background (load average about 4). UDP sender throughput is capped by the harness at about 200 to 230 MB/s with 1 KB datagrams (the direct baseline shows the same cap). The Linux runs are on a 2 vCPU KVM guest, where the harness and Rift compete for the same two cores, so Linux throughput is a lower bound.

Frame size is the harness write unit. For UDP sources it is the datagram size, and Rift forwards one datagram per read. For TCP sources Rift re-chunks into reads of up to 4096 bytes, whatever the frame size.

Overload run: the same soak at 241 MB/s

The first soak attempt offered twice the load: 80 MB/s per TCP direction and 20 MB/s per UDP direction, about 241 MB/s in one process. For the first 4 minutes it delivered everything with slowly growing loss. A burst of host load then pushed it over (an OrbStack restart and a browser download). Delivery fell to 15 to 90 MB/s and never recovered once the host was quiet again, while Rift's CPU went up. Once queues overflow, Rift spends its cycles on drops, lagged broadcast receivers and per-chunk event work, which is classic congestion collapse.

Recommendations

  1. Apply backpressure for stream-to-stream bridges instead of dropping. SideHandle::deliver uses try_send into a 256-slot channel and drops the chunk when it is full (crates/rift-core/src/bridge.rs). For TCP and UART sources, awaiting send would push back through the kernel to the sender, and TCP would flow-control on its own. Keep dropping only for datagram sources, or make it a per-side option such as overflow=drop|block.
  2. Do not drop into the middle of a TCP stream. A dropped 4 KB chunk is not frame-aligned, so the receiver gets a stream with a hole in it, and every protocol on top breaks until it resyncs. If dropping stays, disconnect the TCP peer on overflow instead of silently splicing the stream.
  3. Make queue depth configurable and bigger by default. 256 chunks of 4 KB is about 1 MB, or 5 ms of slack at 200 MB/s. A short scheduler stall is enough to lose data, which is why loss appears at rates far below the ceiling. Size the queue in bytes, not chunks.
  4. Skip the per-chunk event copies when nobody needs them. Every chunk builds a Received and a Forwarded event, each copying the payload into a fresh Vec (event.rs), even with --quiet. Store Bytes in the event, or skip data events when no subscriber wants them. This is most of the about one core per 100 MB/s.
  5. Raise UDP socket buffers. Set SO_RCVBUF/SO_SNDBUF on UDP sides, configurable, so bursts are absorbed by the kernel instead of dropped. The packet-rate ceiling, about 200k datagrams/s, is a recv_from plus channel hop per datagram. recvmmsg batching on Linux would lift it.
  6. Count drops exactly. Drop notifications ride the same broadcast channel as data events, and a lagging subscriber silently skips them. Under overload Rift reported far fewer dropped chunks than were actually lost. Keep a per-side atomic counter and expose it in status.
  7. Exit on SIGTERM even with stdin open. rift pipe/rift run with stdin as an open pipe, for example docker run -i or a supervisor that pipes stdin, does not exit on SIGTERM. The blocking tokio::io::stdin() read keeps the runtime alive until SIGKILL. Use std::process::exit after stop_all, or a runtime shutdown_timeout.
  8. macOS UART: serialport sets the baud with IOSSIOSPEED, which pseudo-terminals reject with ENOTTY. Real USB adapters work, but socat/pty loopbacks for testing do not. Falling back to cfsetspeed for standard rates when IOSSIOSPEED fails would make local testing possible.
